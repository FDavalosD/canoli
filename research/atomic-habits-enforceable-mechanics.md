# Atomic Habits: what it prescribes, and which mechanics software can enforce

Resolves #3. Parent map: #1.

**Question.** What does *Atomic Habits* canonically prescribe, and for each mechanic, can an iPhone app for the Household enforce it, and how?

**Short answer.** The book's system is the Four Laws of Behavior Change plus their inversions. About half of its mechanics can be enforced by software. An app can make implementation intentions, habit stacks, the two-minute rule, tracking, never miss twice, the accountability partner and the habit contract *structurally mandatory*, for example by refusing to save a habit without a time, place and anchor, or by Escalating to the other Member. Environment design, attractiveness and the inversions for bad habits happen in the physical world and inside the Member's head, so the app can only prompt for them, check them or record them. Nothing can make a habit *satisfying* or *attractive*; software can only add the immediate reward or cost the book calls for. One caution matters for Canoli's "force it" premise: Clear says tracking should stay easy, and that a habit should start out tiny. Proof that is too heavy works against the Third Law, so Proof effort needs tuning per habit (see "Tension" below).

Summaries are paraphrased. Chapter numbers refer to James Clear, *Atomic Habits* (Avery, 2018).

## What the book prescribes

### The frame: the habit loop and the Four Laws

- A habit has four stages: cue, craving, response, reward. Each stage gets one law. [AH ch. 3; SUM]
- **Building a good habit:** 1. Make it obvious (cue). 2. Make it attractive (craving). 3. Make it easy (response). 4. Make it satisfying (reward). [AH ch. 3; SUM]
- **Breaking a bad habit (the inversions):** 1. Make it invisible. 2. Make it unattractive. 3. Make it difficult. 4. Make it unsatisfying. [AH ch. 3; SUM]
- Clear grounds the system in identity: each repetition counts as a "vote" for the kind of person you want to be. Systems matter more than goals. [AH ch. 1–2]
- Awareness comes first. The *Habits Scorecard* asks you to list your current daily habits and mark each as good, bad or neutral. [AH ch. 4; RES]

### Mechanics by law

| # | Mechanic | Law | What Clear prescribes (paraphrased) | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Habits Scorecard | 1 | List your existing routine and rate each item. Awareness comes before change. | AH ch. 4; RES |
| 2 | Implementation intention | 1 | State the behaviour, time and location ("I will X at T in L"). An if-then form covers disruptions. | 5 | Self-control is a short-term strategy | inv. 1 | People with strong self-control mostly avoid tempting situations rather than resisting them. Cut the cue, don't white-knuckle through it. | AH ch. 7 |

## Which mechanics an app can enforce

The verdict scale is about what the app can *force*:

- **Fully:** the app can make the mechanic mandatory, or carry it out itself, with no reliance on the Member's honesty beyond Proof.
- **Partially:** the app can require the Member to *declare*, *schedule* or *record* it, but whether it happens in the physical world or in the Member's head can't be verified.
- **Not:** it happens off-device or in the Member's mind. The app can only remind, educate or record.

| # | Mechanic | Enforceable? | What enforcement looks like in Canoli |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | Implementation intention | **Fully** | A habit can't be saved without a behaviour, a time and a location. Each habit also needs an if-then fallback. The app schedules the cue, and it can geofence the location (e.g. "Kitchen") so the nag fires on arrival. |
| 3 | Habit stacking | **Partially → Fully** | Saving a habit requires naming an anchor habit. If the anchor is itself a tracked habit, completing it can trigger the stacked habit's nag at once, which is fully enforceable. If the anchor is off-app (pouring coffee), the app can only fall back to a time window. |
| 11 | Two-minute rule | **Fully** | New habits start as a "gateway" version with a declared tiny scope. The app refuses to "level up" a habit until it has N completions and no double-miss, and it lets Proof start light. |
| 14 | Habit tracking | **Fully** | Proof is the tracking. Each completion is logged with Proof, a chain or streak view is shown, and the number of active habits per Member is capped (Clear recommends 3–4). |
| 15 | Never miss twice | **Fully** | This is a state machine. One miss puts the habit "at risk" and turns up the nag. A second consecutive miss starts Escalation to the other Member. A "bad day" minimum version can count as showing up. |
| 17 | Accountability partner | **Fully** (built in) | The household has two Members, so the other Member is the partner by default. Escalation ends with the partner being told. The partner can see streaks. |
| 18 | Habit contract | **Fully** (in-app) / **Not** (real-world penalty) | The app can require a contract per habit: the habit, the penalty, and signatures from the Member and the partner (an in-app acknowledgement from each phone). It can enforce in-app consequences on breach (tell the partner, record the breach, show the agreed penalty as an owed task). It can't make anyone pay a real-world penalty; the partner and the GTD inbox can only track it as owed. |
| 13 | Immediate reward (cardinal rule) | **Partially** | The app can add instant satisfaction, such as feedback when Proof is accepted, a visible chain, or a partner reaction. It can also add an immediate *cost* to skipping (nags, Escalation). Whether these feel rewarding is subjective. |
| 12 | Commitment devices / automation | **Partially** | The app itself is a commitment device: nags can't be dismissed, and Escalation is pre-agreed. It can't lock the TV or the fridge. It can suggest iOS-level devices (Screen Time, Focus modes) and record that the Member set them up. |
| 1 | Habits Scorecard | **Partially** | Onboarding can require a scorecard (list your routine, rate each item) before habits can be created, and can repeat it at each review. Its accuracy depends on honesty. |
| 20 | Reflection / review | **Partially** | The app can schedule mandatory periodic reviews that can't be skipped, with the partner told if one is missed. This fits the GTD Weekly Review. Whether the reflection is any good is up to the Member. |
| 19 | Goldilocks rule | **Partially** | Completion data shows when a habit is too easy (long, effortless streak) or too hard (repeated misses), so the app can prompt a scale-up or scale-down. It can't measure perceived difficulty. |
| 9 | Repetition over planning | **Partially** | Progress is counted in repetitions, not days. The app can block over-planning, for example by limiting edits before the first rep. |
| 4 | Environment design | **Partially (verify) / Not (perform)** | The app can't rearrange the house. It can ask for an "environment set-up" step when a habit is created (e.g. lay out the running shoes), and Proof can be a photo of the primed environment. Only the Member can actually move things. |
| 10 | Friction (least effort, inverted) | **Partially** | The app can make good-habit logging quick to *open*, but the effort lives in the physical world. For bad habits, it can add friction only where the bad habit is on the phone (Screen Time limits). |
| 5 | Make it invisible (inv. 1) | **Not** | Removing cues is physical or social. The app can only prompt and record. It *can* avoid being a cue for bad habits itself. |
| 6 | Temptation bundling | **Partially** | The app can require the Member to declare a pairing ("I only listen to my podcast while cleaning") and can remind them of it. It can't control the podcast, except where both live on the phone. |
| 7 | Join a culture | **Partially** | Shared habits for both Members and visibility into each other's streaks make the household the "culture". The app can't recruit wider social norms. |
| 8 | Reframe / make it unattractive | **Not** | This is purely mental. The app can show the Member's own written reframe, or the cost of the bad habit, at the moment the cue fires. |
| 16 | Don't measure the wrong thing | **Not** (design guardrail) | This is a caution for Canoli's own design. Proof must measure the behaviour that matters, not something easy to game. |

### Summary by category

- **Fully enforceable (core of a "forcing" app):** implementation intentions, habit tracking via Proof, never miss twice as an Escalation state machine, the accountability partner (the other Member), the habit contract (in-app part), the two-minute rule and gated scaling, and habit stacking when the anchor is in-app.
- **Partially enforceable (the app requires a declaration, verifies by Proof, or adds consequences):** environment set-up (photo Proof), immediate reward and cost, commitment devices, the scorecard, reviews, Goldilocks scaling, temptation bundling, the household as culture, off-app habit stacking.
- **Not enforceable (prompt or record only):** removing cues in the physical world, mental reframing, real-world contract penalties, and whether rewards actually feel satisfying.

## Tension with Canoli's premise

- **Proof vs. the Third Law.** Clear's whole Third Law is about cutting effort, and his tracking advice is to log right away and keep it easy [AH ch. 12–13, 16; HT]. Canoli defines Proof as something that takes real effort. Clear's own framing gives the way out: the effort should go into the *penalty for skipping* (Fourth Law inverted: partner, contract, Escalation [AH ch. 17]), not into the *cost of doing*. Proof should be hard to fake, not hard to give. A photo taken on the spot or a short check-in fits better than a long form. Proof effort could grow with the habit's level, matching the two-minute rule.
- **Nags as cues vs. as punishment.** A scheduled nag at the implementation-intention time is a First Law cue, which is good. Nags that keep going after a miss are Fourth Law inverted punishment. Clear supports both, but [AH ch. 15] warns that the behaviour linked to an immediate unpleasant feeling gets avoided. If the *app itself* becomes the unpleasant thing, Members may start avoiding the app. Design the immediate reward on completion with the same care as the Escalation.
- **Never miss twice as the Escalation threshold.** The book gives a principled default: the first miss stays private and the nag gets stronger; the second consecutive miss tells the partner. This keeps Escalation from being hair-trigger while still "forcing".
